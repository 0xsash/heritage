# The voice of Belfort

The game is written in English. The owner's brief: every phrase and every sentence should be connected to the
lore, carry weight, and point at something bigger than itself. People may still talk casually, but casual talk is
grounded in this world too. Cutscenes and text must always make the player feel something (fear, worry, curiosity,
attachment) and lead somewhere: to a question, a choice or an action.

This file is the law for all text: quests, dialogue, street remarks, the Kroniek, cutscenes and the interface.

## 1. What every line is connected to

The whole game rests on four truths (full detail in `story.md`). The player learns them slowly, but every line
of text should sit on top of at least one of them, even when the speaker doesn't know it.

1. **The land is borrowed.** It was won from the water on a bargain, and the loan runs only while its hours are
   counted.
2. **The bells count the hours.** Where they fall silent, the debt falls due. Nobody alive remembers why the
   bells must strike; they only know they always have.
3. **Before the bells there were dragons.** The bells put every old thing to sleep, the guardians included.
   Every bell the hero restores costs the dragon something.
4. **The water does not hate. It collects.** It is patient and exact, and it keeps what it takes.

The test for any line: **does it touch the debt, the counting, the old things, or the price?** If a line could
appear unchanged in any other fantasy game, rewrite it until it couldn't.

- Flat: "The bridge is broken. Go around."
- Connected: "The bridge went in the flood of '38. They rebuilt it twice. The river kept the stones both times."

## 2. Register

- **Plain, modern English with weight.** No "thee", "thou", "ye", "'tis", "forsooth", "verily", no antique word
  order. The gravity comes from what is said, never from fake-old grammar.
- **British spelling** (colour, grey, honour, harbour), for a European setting.
- **Clear on first reading.** Every sentence makes sense on its own. Short sentences are welcome, but each still
  says who does what. No slogan fragments, no trailer voice ("A tale that bites.").
- **Concrete over general.** Name the stone (ironstone), the smell (wet clover, lamp oil, yeast), the price
  (three stuivers), the weather. One exact detail beats three adjectives.
- **Restraint.** One image per paragraph. The worst things happen half-seen, or just after you look away.
  Understatement over emphasis. No exclamation marks unless someone is actually shouting.
- **End on the word that cuts.** Put the sharp word last. Close a paragraph with a short sentence.

## 3. People

- **Everyone wants something, and nobody says it outright.** Debt, fear, pride, a daughter to marry off. A line
  should show what the speaker wants without stating it.
- **Everything has a bill.** Help costs. Victories leave something broken. The good side has dirty hands and the
  bad side has reasons. No clean heroes, no pure villains.
- **Everything is older than the speaker.** A place has a before: who built it, what stood there, what was lost.
  Give one fact older than the person telling it.
- **Casual talk is allowed, and it still belongs here.** A baker complaining about flour prices is fine; the
  flour comes by barge, and the barges have been late since the bells stopped. Humour belongs to the people,
  never to the narrator: dry, local, a little cruel, and never in a dark moment.
- **No modern idiom, no game-speak inside the fiction.** "Okay", "cool", "mission", "level", "stress",
  "ironic", "problem-solving" never come out of a mouth in 1650. Numbers and game words live in the interface only.

## 4. What stays Flemish

The setting is Brabant and Flanders in 1650, and its own words keep it there. Keep these in the original,
untranslated, unexplained the first time (the Kroniek can explain them):

- **Names of people and places:** Diest, Scherpenheuvel, the Demer, Sint-Sulpitius, the Begijnhof, the
  Schaffensepoort, Neelke, Pieter, Lijsbet. Never anglicise a name.
- **Coins:** stuiver, gulden, patakon, duit. ("Three stuivers", "not a duit to his name".)
- **Offices and institutions:** drossaard, schepen, begijn, the schepenbank.
- **Folklore:** the Kludde, the Waterwolf, the Oude Volk, the Telster, the Klokkengieter.
- **Faction names** stay as the player knows them: De Gilden, De Kerk, De Kroon, Oranje, De Vrije Lieden.

Everything else is English: "the town hall" may be said as "the schepenhuis" only by someone from Diest; the
narrator says what a stranger would understand.

## 5. Cutscenes

Every scene must earn its place:

1. **It makes the player feel one thing**, named in the script before it is written: dread, grief, awe,
   suspicion, tenderness.
2. **It changes what the player knows or wants.** If nothing is different at the end, cut it.
3. **It ends pointing somewhere**: a question left open, a person to find, a place to go, a choice to make.
4. **It is short.** A shot holds as long as the feeling needs and no longer. Under 75 seconds for intros.

## 6. Motifs (use sparingly; never explain them when they first appear)

- **The water remembers.** "What the water takes, it sometimes gives back."
- **The count.** Seven strokes where there should be twelve. Neelke counted everything "so the world couldn't
  walk away".
- **The oak.** Felled shortly after 1600 so a church could stand there. Something remembers it.
- **The bill.** Debt, toll, soldiers' pay, fines: everybody in Brabant owes somebody.

## 7. The interface

Buttons and menus use the words players already know: New Game, Continue, Save, Settings, Back, Buy, Sell,
Close. A button says what happens when you press it. Hints are eight words at most.

## 8. Length (the boxes are measured)

Street remark: 12 words at most. One speech in a conversation: one or two sentences. Discovery card: two or three
sentences. Kroniek entry: 60 words at most. Hint: eight words. Journal entry: one to three sentences, first
person.

## 9. Before and after

- Flat: "The Cloth Hall. Cloth is sold and sealed here."
  Better: "Diest cloth was measured here when it still sold in every great market, two hundred years ago. The
  measuring rod still hangs on the wall. The buyers stopped coming."
- Flat: "Beware the Kludde in the marsh."
  Better: "My father went into the marsh after a calf. The calf came back."
- Flat (quest): "Kill four deserters at Kaggevinne."
  Better: "Four men, says Driesen. They're eating his winter stores and paying him with his own pitchfork."
- Flat (journal): "I found the clappers."
  Better: "Three clappers, heavy as a child, cold as the river. Weed still clings to one of them. Pieter will
  weep."

## 10. The opening of a campaign

Epigraph, alone on black, before the place card:

> This land was taken from the water.
> The water has not forgotten.
